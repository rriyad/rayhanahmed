Brand Messaging Framework
[AI Customer Support Platform]
Internal guide for marketing and sales alignment
Brand VoiceMessaging StrategyB2B Tech
One-Line Positioning

This platform gives support teams the speed of AI with the warmth of a human — so customers always feel heard, not handled.

Who We Are Talking To

Customer success leads at tech companies with 50 to 500 employees. Managing growing ticket volume with a lean team and tired of choosing between fast responses and good ones.

Core Messages by Audience
  • For CS Leads: Cut average response time by 60% without adding headcount or losing your brand's tone.
  • For Support Agents: It handles the repetitive tickets so you can focus on conversations that actually need a human.
  • For Executives: Turn support from a cost center into a retention driver that surfaces insights your product team can act on.
Tone of Voice
  • Confident but not arrogant. We know what we are good at and say it plainly.
  • Practical over polished. Real language, not buzzword soup.
  • Empathetic. We build tools for people who serve other people. That shows in every line.
Words We Never Use
  • Revolutionary, game-changing, or best-in-class — everyone says it, nobody believes it.
  • Leverage synergies — we are not a consulting firm.
  • Simply or just in front of complex claims — it dismisses real work.

AI Workflow

Research to Content in 4 Steps

How I go from a raw brief to a polished content asset in under 30 minutes using Claude. A repeatable process — not a one-off trick.

Workflow Documentation
Brief to Blog Post with AI
Research, outline, draft, and edit — with AI doing the heavy lifting
Prompt EngineeringClaude AIContent Strategy
1

Write a focused brief first

Define the audience, goal, tone, and single key takeaway before touching AI. The quality of the output is only as good as the clarity of the brief.

2

Use a research synthesis prompt

Feed the brief and source material into Claude asking for key insights only — not a summary.

Prompt
You are a content researcher. Based on the brief below, identify the 5 most compelling and non-obvious insights a B2B buyer would find useful. Do not summarize sources. Pull out what is surprising or underreported. Brief: [paste brief here]
Output excerpt
Insight 3: Most churn does not happen at renewal. It happens in the first 30 days when onboarding friction causes silent disengagement — customers stop logging in before they have officially decided to leave...
3

Generate an editorial outline

Prompt Claude to build a scannable outline with a hook, three core sections, and a call to action. Review and reorder manually before drafting.

4

Draft, then edit by hand

Generate a first draft with tone instructions baked in, then edit every paragraph manually. The AI draft is a starting point. Final editing is always done by me so the piece sounds like the brand, not like a language model.

A "COPS" TV crew member was shot and killed in a friendly-fire incident at a Wendy's in Omaha when police opened fire during an attempted robbery, authorities said.

Police said the crew member was struck by an officer's bullet during a chaotic burst of gunfire inside the restaurant, where officers confronted a robbery suspect armed with what turned out to be a realistic airsoft pistol. The suspect was also shot and killed, and the case is under review by the Omaha Police Department's officer-involved investigations squad; a grand jury will also be convened.

Police Chief Todd Schmaderer outlined the shooting at a news conference, offering condolences to the families of both men and saying the department would be "unprecedentedly transparent" as investigators examine the officers' actions.

Schmaderer said the confrontation began when officers were sent to a robbery at a Little Caesar's pizza at 637 N. Saddle Creek Road. Detective Darren Cunningham then radioed from a nearby Wendy's on Dodge Street to report a robbery in progress and called for backup.

Within about 15 seconds, Officers Brooks Riley and Jason Wilhelm arrived, accompanied by cameraman Michael Lee and sound technician Bryce Dion from Langley Productions, producers of "COPS." Cunningham and Riley entered through the east door, followed by Lee, while Wilhelm went in through the west door.

Inside, officers confronted robbery suspect Cortez Washington, 32, who was armed with what appeared to be a handgun. Three witnesses told investigators they saw Washington firing directly at Cunningham and Riley and heard the first two shots come from the suspect's gun.

The officers returned fire. Washington fled out the east doors before collapsing in the parking lot, where he was handcuffed and later pronounced dead at the University of Nebraska Medical Center. His weapon was recovered and found to be an airsoft pistol — firing plastic pellets but closely resembling a real firearm.

"Nobody wanted Bryce to get hurt. Nobody wanted anybody to get hurt. Police work is very dangerous. It's very chaotic."
— Police Chief Todd Schmaderer

Dion, 38, was struck by a single round in the front vestibule near the east entrance. He was wearing a ballistic vest, but the bullet entered through his left arm and slipped between an open area of the vest. He was pronounced dead at the University of Nebraska Medical Center.

Schmaderer confirmed the shooting was friendly fire. "It was an officer's round that struck Mr. Dion," he said. The three officers who fired were placed on paid administrative leave — standard procedure whenever an officer discharges a weapon and injury or death occurs.

Executive producer Morgan Langley described Dion as one of the company's most experienced employees. "Bryce has been with us for seven years, and honestly, he really is one of our best," Langley said.

Opinion Writing

Point of View

An original opinion piece written in my own voice on a topic I think about seriously. This one is not spec work — it is how I actually see the world.

Opinion — Original Writing
AI Is Not the Threat. Refusing to Adapt Is.
On why the fear of AI says more about us than it does about the technology
Original WritingOpinionAI and Work

Every major technological shift in history has been met with the same response — fear first, adaptation second, dependency third. The printing press threatened scribes. Automobiles threatened horse traders. The internet threatened every industry that had not figured out it was already obsolete. AI is no different, except that this time the resistance is louder, faster, and considerably more dramatic.

The conversation around AI is full of mixed signals. On one side, organizations are racing to integrate it into every workflow they can find. On the other, individuals are treating it like an existential threat to their livelihoods, their relevance, and in some cases their identity. Both reactions are understandable. Neither is particularly useful.

"The issue is not that AI is too powerful. The issue is that too many people are waiting for someone else to figure out what to do with it."

Here is what I actually believe: AI is a toolbox. An extraordinary, fast-evolving, occasionally overwhelming toolbox — but a toolbox nonetheless. A hammer does not replace a carpenter. It makes the carpenter more capable. The people treating AI as a replacement are misunderstanding what it does. The people treating it as a threat are misunderstanding what they do.

What concerns me more than AI itself is the gap it is quietly creating. The professionals who engage with it seriously — who learn to prompt well, think critically about its outputs, and build workflows around its strengths — are pulling ahead. The ones who avoid it are not staying still. They are falling behind at the pace AI is moving forward. That gap is going to be very difficult to close in three years. Nearly impossible in five.

This is not about becoming a machine learning engineer or understanding how transformers work. It is about developing a minimum threshold of AI literacy — knowing which tools exist, what they are good at, where they fail, and how to direct them toward real problems. That threshold is not high. But you have to choose to cross it.

Industries do not wait for everyone to catch up. They restructure around whoever adapted first. The professionals who frame AI as a threat are not wrong that change is coming — they are just drawing the wrong conclusion from it. The threat is not the technology. The threat is the widening distance between the people who decided to learn and the people who decided to wait.

We all have the same window of time to close that distance. The question is whether we use it.
